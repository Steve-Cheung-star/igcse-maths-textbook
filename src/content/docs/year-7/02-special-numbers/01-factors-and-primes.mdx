## 1. Inquiry: The Building Blocks of Numbers

**Inquiry Questions:** 
* How can number patterns help us learn more about numbers?
* Why is every number greater than $1$ either a prime or made of primes?

### Key Vocabulary
* **Integer:** A whole number (e.g., $-3, 0, 5$).
* **Factor (Divisor):** A number that divides into another exactly. For example, $3$ is a factor of $12$.
* **Multiple:** The product of a number and any integer. For example, $10, 20,$ and $30$ are multiples of $10$.
* **Prime Number:** A number with exactly two factors: $1$ and itself.
* **Composite Number:** A number with more than two factors.
</section>

## 2. Divisibility Rules

Before finding factors, it helps to know if a number divides evenly by another without actually doing the long division. Here are some quick rules to spot factors!

<div className="table-wrapper">
| Number | Divisibility Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **2** | The last digit is even ($0, 2, 4, 6, 8$). | $1,234$ ends in $4$, so it is divisible by $2$. |
| **3** | The sum of all digits is a multiple of $3$. | $147 \rightarrow 1 + 4 + 7 = 12$ ($12$ is a multiple of $3$). |
| **4** | The last two digits form a multiple of $4$. | $3,516 \rightarrow 16$ is a multiple of $4$. |
| **5** | The last digit is $0$ or $5$. | $3,495$ ends in $5$. |
| **9** | The sum of all digits is a multiple of $9$. | $819 \rightarrow 8 + 1 + 9 = 18$ ($18$ is a multiple of $9$). |
| **10** | The last digit is $0$. | $540$ ends in $0$. |
</div>

<SteveTip title="Exam Strategy: Divisibility">
Always check divisibility by 2, 3, and 5 first when dealing with large numbers in an exam. It will save you precious time when breaking down numbers!
</SteveTip>
</section>

## 3. Prime Factor Decomposition

Every **composite number** can be uniquely expressed as a product of prime factors. We call this "Prime Factor Decomposition." You can think of prime numbers as the "LEGO bricks" of mathematics.

<Aside type="note" title="The Factor Tree Strategy">
When building a factor tree, it doesn't matter which factors you start with; as long as you keep branching until you hit primes, the final "leaves" will always be exactly the same.
</Aside>
</section>

## 4. HCF and LCM

Once we have the prime factors, we can solve complex problems involving shared numbers.
* **Highest Common Factor (HCF):** The largest number that is a factor of two or more numbers.
* **Lowest Common Multiple (LCM):** The smallest number that is a multiple of two or more numbers.

<Aside type="tip" title="Venn Diagrams for HCF and LCM">
You can use a Venn diagram of the prime factors to easily find HCF and LCM. 
* **HCF** = Multiply the numbers in the *overlap* (intersection).
* **LCM** = Multiply *all* the numbers in the entire diagram (union).
</Aside>
</section>

<details className="styled-details">
<summary>View the calculation steps</summary>
<Steps>
1. Prime factorise $12$: $12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3$.
2. Prime factorise $18$: $18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3$.
3. Find the common factors (the overlap in the Venn diagram): One '$2$' and one '$3$'.
4. Calculate **HCF**: Multiply the overlap. $2 \times 3 = 6$.
5. Calculate **LCM**: Multiply all numbers in the diagram. $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 36$.
</Steps>

### Q3: Prime Factorization
<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="📝 Question">
    Write the prime factorization (using index notation where possible) for:
    
    a) $20$  
    b) $24$  
    c) $42$  
    d) $75$  
    e) $144$  
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="✅ Solution">
    a) **20:** $2 \times 2 \times 5 = 2^2 \times 5$  
    b) **24:** $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 = 2^3 \times 3$  
    c) **42:** $2 \times 3 \times 7$  
    d) **75:** $3 \times 5 \times 5 = 3 \times 5^2$  
    e) **144:** $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 2^4 \times 3^2$  
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

<section className="lesson-chunk">
### Q4: Real-World HCF and LCM
<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="📝 Question">
    **Part A:** Bus A leaves the station every $15$ minutes. Bus B leaves every $25$ minutes. If they both leave the station together at 9:00 AM, at what time will they next leave the station together?
    
    **Part B:** A florist has $36$ roses and $60$ tulips. She wants to create identical bouquets with no flowers left over. What is the greatest number of bouquets she can make?
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="✅ Solution">
    **Part A:** We need the LCM of $15$ and $25$. 
    $15 = 3 \times 5$ and $25 = 5^2$. LCM = $3 \times 5^2 = 75$ minutes. 
    $75$ minutes after 9:00 AM is **10:15 AM**.

    **Part B:** We need the HCF of $36$ and $60$. 
    $36 = 2^2 \times 3^2$ and $60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5$. 
    The shared prime factors are $2^2 \times 3 = 12$. She can make **12 bouquets**.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>
</section>

<section className="lesson-chunk">
### Q5: The Prime Puzzle Challenge
<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="📝 Question">
    Investigate the following two puzzles:
    
    **Puzzle A:** I am thinking of two numbers. Their HCF is $4$ and their LCM is $48$. Both numbers are smaller than $20$. What are the numbers?
    
    **Puzzle B:** The product of two different prime numbers is $143$. What is the sum of these two prime numbers?
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="✅ Solution">
    **Puzzle A:** Multiples of $4$ under $20$ are $4, 8, 12, 16$. Testing pairs for an LCM of $48$ gives **12 and 16**.
    
    **Puzzle B:** Finding the prime factors of $143$: Since $143 \div 11 = 13$, the two prime numbers are $11$ and $13$. The sum is $11 + 13 = \mathbf{24}$.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>
