<section className="lesson-chunk">
Rounding makes long or complex numbers easier to work with by keeping them close to their original value but simpler to write. Whether you are estimating the cost of a multi-million pound project or calculating the length of a triangle to 3 significant figures, rounding is a core skill. In IGCSE Maths, providing your final answer to the correct degree of accuracy is often worth a dedicated accuracy mark in the exam!
</section>

## 1. The Golden Rule of Rounding

Whether you are rounding to a place value, a decimal place, or a significant figure, the fundamental mechanism is always the same: identify your target digit, then **look at the very next digit to the right**.

* **0, 1, 2, 3, or 4:** Round **down** (keep the target digit exactly the same).
* **5, 6, 7, 8, or 9:** Round **up** (increase the target digit by 1).

### Rounding Large Numbers (Millions, Thousands, Hundreds)

When dealing with populations, distances in space, or national budgets, we often round to the nearest million or thousand. The rule remains identical, but any digits to the right of your target place value turn into **placeholding zeroes**.

<div className="table-wrapper">

| Original Number | Nearest 10 | Nearest 1 000 | Nearest 1 000 000 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **8 529 481** | 8 529 480 | 8 529 000 | 9 000 000 |
| **3 499 502** | 3 499 500 | 3 500 000 | 3 000 000 |
| **12 050 962**| 12 050 960| 12 051 000| 12 000 000|

</div>
</section>

<section className="lesson-chunk">
**Worked Example 1: Rounding Place Values**  
* **a)** Round **6 839 521** to the nearest million.
* **b)** Round **14 962** to the nearest thousand.
* **c)** Round **1 095 400** to the nearest ten thousand.

<details className="styled-details">
<summary>View step-by-step solutions</summary>
* **a) 7 000 000:** The millions digit is 6. The decider digit is 8. Since 8 is 5 or more, round up the 6 to a 7. Replace the rest with zeroes.
* **b) 15 000:** The thousands digit is 4. The decider digit is 9. Round up the 4 to a 5. Replace the rest with zeroes.
* **c) 1 100 000:** The ten thousands digit is 9. The decider digit is 5. Round up the 9, which cascades and turns the 0 into a 1. Replace the rest with zeroes.
</details>

<Aside type="tip" title="The Cascade Effect (Rounding 9s)">
If your target digit is a 9 and you need to round up, the 9 becomes a 0 and you must carry the 1 to the next column to the left. 
For example, rounding `3 982` to the nearest hundred: the `8` tells you to round up the `9`. The `9` becomes a `0`, and the `3` becomes a `4`, giving **4 000**.
</Aside>
</section>

## 2. Decimal Places (d.p.)

Rounding to decimal places focuses entirely on the position of the digit relative to the decimal point. You simply count the required number of places to the right of the decimal point, look at the very next digit to decide whether to round up, and drop everything that follows.

* **1 d.p.** means keep one digit after the decimal point.
* **2 d.p.** means keep two digits after the decimal point.
* **3 d.p.** means keep three digits after the decimal point.
</section>

<section className="lesson-chunk">
**Worked Example 2: Rounding to Decimal Places**  
* **a)** Round **8 542.361** to 1 d.p.
* **b)** Round **0.004 29** to 2 d.p.
* **c)** Round **14.996** to 2 d.p.

<details className="styled-details">
<summary>View step-by-step solutions</summary>
* **a) 8 542.4:** The target digit is 3 (8 542.**3**61). The decider is 6, so we round up. The 3 becomes a 4.
* **b) 0.00:** The target digit is the second 0 (0.0**0**4 29). The decider is 4, which is less than 5, so we round down.
* **c) 15.00:** The target digit is the second 9 (14.9**9**6). The decider is 6, so we round up. This cascades to make 15.00.
</details>

<SteveTip title="Keep Your Zeroes!">
If you are asked to round to 2 d.p., your final answer **must** have exactly two numbers after the decimal point—even if the last number is a zero!
As seen in Example 2c, the answer is **15.00**. Do not write 15. You need those zeroes to prove you measured to the required level of accuracy.
</SteveTip>
</section>

## 3. Significant Figures (s.f.)

Significant figures measure the overall precision of a number, regardless of where the decimal point sits. This is the most common way to round in science and higher-level maths.

### The Golden Rules of Significant Figures:
1. Start counting from the **first non-zero digit** (reading left to right). This is your 1st significant figure.
2. Once you start counting, **all** digits count as significant (including trapped zeroes).
3. Leading zeroes (like those in `0.005`) are purely placeholders and are **never** significant.

<section className="lesson-chunk">
**Worked Example 3: Rounding to Significant Figures**  
* **a)** Round **0.000 820 6** to 2 s.f.
* **b)** Round **1 050 321** to 3 s.f.
* **c)** Round **0.049 98** to 3 s.f.

<details className="styled-details">
<summary>View step-by-step solutions</summary>
* **a) 0.000 82:** Ignore the leading zeroes. The first significant digit is 8. The target digit (2nd s.f.) is 2. The decider is 0, so round down.
* **b) 1 050 000:** The first digit is 1. The target digit (3rd s.f.) is 5. The decider is 0, so we do not round up. Replace the rest of the integer with placeholder zeroes.
* **c) 0.050 0:** The first significant digit is 4. The target digit (3rd s.f.) is the second 9. The decider is 8, so round up. This cascades, making the 4 a 5. *(Keep the trailing zero to show exactly 3 s.f. precision).*
</details>

<SteveTip title="The Placeholder Trap">
When rounding large whole numbers to significant figures, **do not accidentally shrink the number**.
If you round `5 320 000` to 1 significant figure, the answer is **5 000 000**, NOT 5. You must replace the dropped digits before the decimal point with zeroes to hold the place value!
</SteveTip>
